POLARIS EMS Stakeholder Policy Decisions
Role-Based Proposal — Polar Station Research Manager
Role: POLAR_STATION_RESEARCH_MANAGER Status: PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION — NOT FORMALLY APPROVED Scope: 16 unresolved EMS stakeholder policy decisions Governance note: These answers are a stakeholder input to the POLARIS governance process. They do not authorize any code, MILP, or objective-function change.

1. Executive Summary — Proposed Station-Management Philosophy
The station's energy management system exists to keep people alive and safe first, keep the station's core mission (communications, safety systems, science continuity) running second, and conserve diesel third — but diesel conservation is never allowed to threaten the first two. Diesel is a finite, hard-to-resupply resource, but it is also the station's insurance policy against renewable variability and polar night. The philosophy proposed here is:

Reliability of critical infrastructure is a near-hard constraint, not a price-weighted trade-off. The EMS should not be willing to sacrifice meaningful critical-load service to save modest amounts of fuel. This directly contradicts treating unmet load as a soft economic penalty at whatever coefficient a solver finds convenient (e.g., the current $10,000/kWh vs. $0.625/kWh implementation ratio, which is a software artifact, not a stakeholder decision).
Diesel minimization is a secondary, structurally bounded objective: minimize diesel subject to reliability, safety, and reserve constraints — not diesel-minimize with reliability as a penalty term competing on the same numerical scale.
Battery reserves exist for two distinct purposes — a technical minimum (equipment protection) and an operational emergency reserve (people/mission protection) — and these must not be conflated.
Forecast uncertainty should be handled conservatively, with explicit, bounded reserve margins rather than blind trust in point forecasts, especially for solar during polar-night transitions and for wind during storm-prone periods.
Curtailment of renewable energy is an acceptable, sometimes necessary, operational outcome — not a failure — when it protects batteries, respects generator minimum-output constraints, or preserves reserve margins.
All numeric values proposed below are provisional stakeholder starting points, explicitly flagged for engineering validation against actual station load data, battery chemistry/degradation curves, generator specifications, fuel logistics, and site-specific weather/forecast performance.
This is a policy philosophy proposal, not an engineering specification, and not an approved MILP objective.

2. Detailed Answers to the 16 Decisions
DECISION 01 — Critical Load Classification
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Adopt a five-tier load classification: CRITICAL, ESSENTIAL, DEFERRABLE, INTERRUPTIBLE, NONCRITICAL. Not all scientific load is critical; classification is by consequence of loss, not by disciplinary importance.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

CRITICAL — loss creates immediate risk to human life or irreversible loss of core station safety capability. Examples: life support systems, personnel-safety heating (minimum habitability temperature in occupied structures), emergency lighting, fire detection/suppression, primary and backup communications (including distress/emergency comms), medical equipment, and the control systems of the EMS itself.
ESSENTIAL — loss does not immediately endanger life but materially threatens station habitability, food/water safety, or irrecoverable scientific/infrastructure loss within hours. Examples: food storage refrigeration/freezers, potable water systems, primary ventilation, core network/data infrastructure needed for safety monitoring, and scientific experiments/samples that are irreplaceable if power is lost (e.g., long-duration environmental samples, ongoing multi-year measurement series) — but only those specifically designated irreplaceable, not all science by default.
DEFERRABLE — loads whose operation can be delayed hours to a day or two without loss of value. Examples: routine scientific instruments with restart tolerance, non-time-critical data processing, battery charging for non-essential equipment, scheduled maintenance power draws.
INTERRUPTIBLE — loads that can be shed for the duration of a shortage and resumed later with no lasting harm. Examples: workshop/shop power, non-essential heating in unoccupied structures, laundry, general-purpose outlets, most comfort loads.
NONCRITICAL — discretionary/comfort loads with no operational consequence from shedding. Examples: recreational electronics, non-essential lighting, decorative or convenience loads.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: NONE (classification scheme, not a numeric policy) UNIT: NONE TIME_SCOPE: Continuous classification, reviewed seasonally APPLIES_TO: All station electrical loads EXCEPTIONS: Station manager/safety officer may temporarily reclassify a load (e.g., elevate a scientific load to ESSENTIAL for a defined experiment window) with documented justification. RATIONALE: A station cannot operate as if every load is critical — that collapses the EMS's ability to make meaningful trade-offs. But undervaluing life-support and comms is unacceptable. A tiered scheme lets the EMS shed gracefully instead of failing catastrophically or treating everything as equally protected. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — actual load inventory must be mapped to these tiers. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES — verify tier assignments against real circuit-level metering/labeling. FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 02 — Load Shedding Classification
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: CRITICAL loads must never be automatically shed by the EMS except in a declared CRITICAL_INFRASTRUCTURE_EMERGENCY or BATTERY_EMERGENCY mode, and even then only as an absolute last resort after all lower tiers have been shed. ESSENTIAL loads may be shed automatically only after all DEFERRABLE and INTERRUPTIBLE loads are shed. DEFERRABLE and INTERRUPTIBLE loads may be shed or deferred automatically by the EMS under normal operating rules.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

Never automatically shed (absent declared emergency): CRITICAL loads.
May be shed only in a declared emergency mode, and only after all lower tiers are exhausted: CRITICAL loads, as a last-resort measure, with mandatory operator notification (not necessarily prior operator approval if truly life-safety-time-critical, but immediate notification and logging).
May be shed automatically under defined trigger conditions (e.g., low SOC, fuel constraint) without special emergency declaration: ESSENTIAL (only after DEFERRABLE/INTERRUPTIBLE are shed), DEFERRABLE, INTERRUPTIBLE.
Always shed first, and freely: NONCRITICAL.
Restoration priority on recovery: reverse of shedding order — CRITICAL first (already never lost under normal ops), then ESSENTIAL, DEFERRABLE, INTERRUPTIBLE, NONCRITICAL — restored as generation/battery capacity becomes available, with a minimum dwell time (proposed 5 minutes, PROVISIONAL) before re-shedding the same load to avoid oscillation ("chattering").
Automated load shedding is authorized for DEFERRABLE/INTERRUPTIBLE/NONCRITICAL tiers without operator-in-the-loop approval, subject to logging and after-the-fact review.
ESSENTIAL load shedding requires either automated rule-based triggers pre-approved by the station manager, or real-time operator concurrence where communications/time permit; if operator is unreachable and conditions are worsening, automated shedding is authorized with mandatory notification as soon as possible.
CRITICAL load shedding always requires either prior operator declaration of emergency mode, or is auto-executed only under an unambiguous, pre-defined last-resort trigger (e.g., battery SOC at technical minimum with no other loads left to shed and diesel generation unavailable) — this must be logged, alarmed, and reported immediately.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: 5 (minimum restoration dwell time, PROVISIONAL) UNIT: minutes TIME_SCOPE: Per shedding/restoration event APPLIES_TO: All tiers EXCEPTIONS: Generator/equipment protection trips (hardware-level, not EMS-level) always override software shedding logic. RATIONALE: Graceful degradation prevents an all-or-nothing failure. Reserving automatic CRITICAL-load shedding for true last-resort, well-defined conditions protects human safety while still allowing the EMS to respond fast enough to prevent total blackout in an extreme event. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — actual switching hardware/relay capability must support this granularity. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 03 — Minimum Critical-Load Service Requirement
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: 100% continuous energy service to CRITICAL loads during NORMAL_OPERATION and FORECAST_DEGRADED modes; a defined, bounded, non-zero allowance for unmet CRITICAL load exists only during declared BATTERY_EMERGENCY or CRITICAL_INFRASTRUCTURE_EMERGENCY modes (equipment failure, catastrophic weather, etc.), where the requirement shifts to "best achievable service, documented and reviewed."

POLICY_DEFINITION:

METRIC: Energy served as a fraction of energy demanded by CRITICAL-tier loads (kWh served / kWh demanded), measured continuously.
TARGET: 100% during NORMAL_OPERATION and FORECAST_DEGRADED modes. During declared emergencies, no fixed percentage target is imposed — the goal becomes "minimize unmet CRITICAL-load energy given the actual constraint," measured and reported after the fact.
TIME_WINDOW: Continuous (instantaneous) service is the target; rolling daily and monthly energy-served ratios are also reported as a station-health metric.
EXCEPTIONS: Declared emergencies (equipment failure beyond redundancy, catastrophic weather exceeding design envelope, or diesel logistics failure combined with renewable failure) are explicitly exempted from the 100% target, but every such event must be logged, root-caused, and reported to governance.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: 100 (normal/degraded modes); NONE fixed (emergency mode — best-achievable, reported) UNIT: % of CRITICAL-load energy served TIME_SCOPE: Continuous, with daily/monthly rollups APPLIES_TO: CRITICAL tier only EXCEPTIONS: As above. RATIONALE: A blanket "100% always" target ignores that catastrophic events can exceed any station's physical capability; pretending otherwise produces either an infeasible optimization or a dishonest policy. Separating "target" (normal ops) from "best effort, reported" (true emergencies) keeps the policy honest and enforceable. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — actual CRITICAL load profile needed to validate feasibility of 100% target under worst-case renewable/diesel scenarios. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 04 — Maximum Acceptable Unmet Critical Load
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Zero unmet CRITICAL load during NORMAL_OPERATION and FORECAST_DEGRADED modes (hard constraint, not a priced trade-off). During declared emergency/catastrophic conditions, unmet CRITICAL load is bounded operationally (by shedding order and generator/battery physics) rather than by a pre-set numeric ceiling, but every kWh of unmet CRITICAL load must be logged and reported, with a post-event review required if cumulative unmet CRITICAL load in a rolling 30-day period exceeds a provisional review-trigger threshold.

POLICY_DEFINITION: This is explicitly NOT expressed as a dollar-per-kWh penalty (rejecting the current $10,000/kWh implementation coefficient as the stakeholder answer). Instead:

NORMAL_OPERATION / FORECAST_DEGRADED: Unmet CRITICAL load = 0 kWh (hard constraint the EMS must not violate by choice; the EMS must instead draw down battery, run diesel, or shed lower tiers to avoid it).
EMERGENCY / CATASTROPHIC conditions (equipment failure, extreme weather beyond design limits, fuel exhaustion): No numeric ceiling is imposed; the requirement is "minimize unmet CRITICAL load given the physical constraint" plus mandatory logging.
Review trigger (PROVISIONAL): Any single event with more than 15 minutes of continuous unmet CRITICAL load, or cumulative unmet CRITICAL load exceeding 2 kWh in a rolling 30-day period, triggers mandatory governance review — not because 2 kWh is inherently catastrophic, but because it signals the reliability policy may be failing structurally.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: 0 (normal/degraded); 15 / 2 (review triggers, PROVISIONAL) UNIT: kWh unmet (0 target); minutes (event trigger); kWh/30 days (cumulative trigger) TIME_SCOPE: Continuous target; 30-day rolling review window APPLIES_TO: CRITICAL tier EXCEPTIONS: Declared EMERGENCY/CATASTROPHIC modes only. RATIONALE: A dollar-denominated penalty invites the optimizer to "buy" unmet critical load whenever fuel savings exceed the arbitrary price — that is exactly the wrong incentive structure for life-safety loads. A hard constraint with an explicit, narrow emergency carve-out is more honest and operationally enforceable, and the review triggers make the policy self-auditing without pretending catastrophic risk can be priced away. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — review-trigger thresholds should be calibrated to actual CRITICAL load magnitude and historical event frequency. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 05 — Maximum Continuous Interruption
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Differentiated maximum continuous interruption durations by tier, with CRITICAL loads held to the shortest tolerances consistent with realistic switching/backup equipment behavior.

POLICY_DEFINITION (PROVISIONAL, pending equipment validation):

CRITICAL: 0 seconds target (uninterrupted); if physically unavoidable (e.g., transfer switching), maximum tolerated gap = 5 seconds, bridged by local UPS/battery buffering at the load level, not solely by the station EMS.
ESSENTIAL: Maximum continuous interruption = 30 minutes under normal/degraded operation.
DEFERRABLE: Maximum continuous interruption = 8 hours (roughly half a working shift) before requiring operator review of extended deferral.
INTERRUPTIBLE: Maximum continuous interruption = 24 hours before mandatory reassessment; may be shed indefinitely during extended low-resource periods with periodic (daily) operator review.
NONCRITICAL: No enforced maximum; may remain shed for the duration of a resource-constrained period.
Emergency exceptions: During declared EMERGENCY/CATASTROPHIC modes, these limits are targets, not guarantees; the EMS should still attempt to minimize interruption duration for each tier but is not required to meet the stated limits if physically infeasible.

NUMERICAL_VALUE: 0 (target)/5 (max, CRITICAL); 30 (ESSENTIAL); 480 (DEFERRABLE); 1440 (INTERRUPTIBLE) UNIT: seconds (CRITICAL); minutes (ESSENTIAL, DEFERRABLE, INTERRUPTIBLE) TIME_SCOPE: Per interruption event APPLIES_TO: All tiers, differentiated EXCEPTIONS: Declared emergency modes relax these as best-effort targets. RATIONALE: Treating all loads as needing "instant, always-on" power is unrealistic and would force oversized battery/generator capacity; treating all loads as tolerant of arbitrary interruption risks safety and mission loss. Differentiated tolerances let the EMS make load-shedding decisions that match real operational consequence. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — actual switching hardware, local UPS presence, and equipment restart behavior (e.g., can lab freezers tolerate a 30-minute gap without sample loss?) must be verified per load. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 06 — Reliability Formulation
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Adopt a hybrid hard-constraint + lexicographic formulation (Option C/D combined): CRITICAL-load reliability is enforced as a hard constraint during normal/degraded operation; when the hard constraint becomes infeasible (a true emergency), the EMS falls back to a lexicographic priority order — not a single soft penalty traded against fuel cost on the same objective scale.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

What must never happen (hard constraint, normal/degraded modes): Any unmet CRITICAL load, any violation of the technical minimum battery SOC (equipment protection), any generator operation outside safe limits.
What can be traded off: Diesel consumption vs. renewable curtailment vs. battery cycling vs. lower-tier load shedding — these are optimized against each other within the objective function, subject to the hard constraints above.
What happens during infeasibility (true emergency — hard constraints cannot all be satisfied simultaneously): The EMS switches to a declared EMERGENCY mode and applies a lexicographic fallback: (1) preserve human safety systems and comms above all else, even shedding other CRITICAL loads if forced to choose; (2) minimize unmet CRITICAL-load energy; (3) minimize duration of any CRITICAL interruption; (4) then optimize fuel/renewable/battery use among remaining feasible options. This avoids collapsing all these priorities into one dollar-weighted number, which can produce non-intuitive and operationally indefensible decisions (e.g., an optimizer "choosing" to black out communications to save a small amount of diesel because a penalty coefficient happened to make that numerically attractive).
Emergency operation: Explicitly defined as a distinct EMS operating mode with its own rule set (see Decision 15 and operating-mode definitions), not merely a different penalty weight in the same objective.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: NONE (formulation choice) UNIT: NONE TIME_SCOPE: NONE APPLIES_TO: Entire EMS objective structure EXCEPTIONS: NONE beyond declared emergency fallback described above. RATIONALE: A purely soft-penalty formulation (like the current $10,000/kWh vs $0.625/kWh implementation) makes reliability a function of an arbitrary numeric ratio chosen by software engineers, not station operational judgment — and small changes to that ratio can produce large, unpredictable changes in real-world reliability behavior. A pure hard-constraint formulation, on the other hand, can become infeasible and offer no graceful guidance when the constraint truly cannot be met. The hybrid approach gets the best of both: strict protection in the 99%+ of situations where it's achievable, and clear, explainable, human-reviewable behavior in true emergencies. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: NO (formulation is policy-level) ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES — MILP/solver implementation of hard constraints + lexicographic fallback needs engineering design and testing. FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 07 — Definition of "Minimize Diesel Use"
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Primary metric: liters of diesel fuel consumed (not kWh generated, not cost, not emissions) — because fuel volume is the station's actual physical resupply/logistics constraint. Secondary considerations tracked but not primary-optimized: generator runtime (maintenance burden), generator start count (wear/reliability), and fuel cost (informational, not primary driver at a remote station where resupply access matters more than price).

POLICY_DEFINITION:

Primary metric: Total liters of diesel consumed per defined period (daily/monthly/annual), because this maps directly to physical fuel inventory and resupply planning — the binding real-world constraint at a remote polar station.
Secondary metric 1 — Generator runtime (hours): tracked because excessive runtime accelerates maintenance intervals and part wear, which is itself a logistics risk (spare parts, technician availability).
Secondary metric 2 — Generator start count: tracked because frequent starts/stops cause more mechanical wear than sustained runtime and can indicate an EMS dispatch pattern that is inefficient even if total liters look acceptable.
Secondary metric 3 — Fuel cost ($): tracked for budgeting but explicitly NOT the primary driver, since at a remote station the marginal $/liter figure understates the true cost of running out of fuel or needing an emergency resupply.
Explicitly NOT treated as equivalent: liters consumed, kWh of diesel-generated energy, and generator runtime are related but distinct — e.g., a generator running at partial load burns fuel less efficiently per kWh, so "minimize diesel kWh" and "minimize liters" can diverge. Liters is the ground-truth resource constraint and is the chosen primary metric.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: NONE (metric definition, not a target value — see Decision 08 for the ceiling) UNIT: Liters (primary); hours, start-count, $ (secondary/informational) TIME_SCOPE: Daily operational tracking; monthly and annual rollups for logistics planning APPLIES_TO: Diesel generation subsystem EXCEPTIONS: During EMERGENCY modes, diesel minimization is explicitly subordinated to reliability (see Decisions 06, 14, 15). RATIONALE: At a remote polar station, the thing that actually runs out and is hard to replace is physical fuel volume, not an abstract cost figure. Optimizing directly for liters keeps the EMS's fuel objective aligned with the real logistics constraint rather than a proxy that can diverge from it (e.g., cost-minimizing could favor high diesel kWh output if diesel were "cheap" in the objective, while liters-minimizing correctly captures the physical scarcity). STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — generator fuel-consumption curves (L/kWh at various load points) needed to make liters-based optimization accurate. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 08 — Fuel Ceiling / Budget
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Yes, POLARIS should have an explicit fuel ceiling, expressed as a rolling monthly consumption budget plus a protected emergency reserve that is not drawn down for routine operations. Both figures are PROVISIONAL — REQUIRES STATION DATA, since actual storage capacity, resupply schedule, and worst-case isolation duration have not been supplied.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

Normal operating budget: A monthly liters-consumed ceiling calibrated to (annual resupply volume − emergency reserve) ÷ (number of resupply-interval months), reviewed and reset each resupply cycle. No specific liter figure is proposed here because actual storage capacity and resupply frequency were not provided.
Emergency reserve: A fixed volume of fuel held back and excluded from the normal operating budget, intended to cover a defined worst-case extended low-renewable / delayed-resupply period. Proposed provisional sizing: enough fuel to run CRITICAL + ESSENTIAL loads for a minimum of 30 days at winter/polar-night average load, as a starting policy target — explicitly flagged PROVISIONAL — REQUIRES STATION DATA (actual load profile, generator efficiency, and historical resupply reliability are needed to validate 30 days as the right number vs. a shorter or longer figure).
Status during normal operation: The monthly ceiling is a soft-managed budget (informs dispatch decisions, triggers review if exceeded) — not a hard MILP constraint that would force CRITICAL load shedding purely to stay under a liters number.
Status during emergency: The emergency reserve may be drawn upon only with station-manager (or delegated on-duty authority) authorization, and drawing on it triggers a mandatory resupply-planning review.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: 30 (emergency reserve sizing, PROVISIONAL — REQUIRES STATION DATA); monthly ceiling value NOT specified (depends on unknown storage/resupply data) UNIT: days of CRITICAL+ESSENTIAL load coverage (emergency reserve); liters/month (normal ceiling, value TBD) TIME_SCOPE: Monthly (operating budget); annual/resupply-cycle (reserve sizing basis) APPLIES_TO: Diesel fuel inventory, station-wide EXCEPTIONS: Emergency reserve may be tapped only under declared emergency with recorded authorization. RATIONALE: An unbounded "use diesel whenever the optimizer wants" approach risks depleting fuel before the next resupply, especially given unpredictable weather delaying resupply vessels/flights. A soft monthly budget with a protected hard-walled emergency reserve balances operational flexibility against the catastrophic risk of running out of fuel during isolation. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — storage tank capacity, resupply schedule/reliability, actual winter load profile, generator efficiency curves. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 09 — Renewable Utilization Target
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Yes, adopt a renewable utilization target, defined as renewable energy actually served to load as a fraction of total energy served (not generation potential, not installed capacity), measured on a rolling monthly basis with separate expectations for polar-day vs. polar-night periods. No fixed year-round percentage is proposed as "correct" — instead a seasonally differentiated provisional target is proposed.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

METRIC: (Renewable kWh served to load) ÷ (Total kWh served to load), over a rolling monthly window. This is chosen over "renewable energy generated" because generation that is curtailed or lost to battery-charge limits doesn't actually reduce diesel use — utilization-to-load is what matters operationally.
TIME_WINDOW: Monthly rolling average, reported separately for polar-day-dominant months vs. polar-night-dominant months, because the two are not comparable and a single annual number would obscure operationally important seasonal reality.
PROVISIONAL TARGETS (require validation against actual resource data and load profile): Polar-day months: renewable utilization target of 50–70% of served load (wide range reflecting genuine uncertainty about site resource without station-specific solar/wind data). Polar-night months: no minimum renewable utilization target is proposed — during this period diesel and battery-stored energy are expected to dominate, and forcing a renewable percentage would be physically unrealistic and could compromise reliability.
NORMAL vs EMERGENCY: The target is explicitly a secondary objective — it must never be pursued in a way that increases unmet CRITICAL load risk, displaces necessary reserve charging, or forces excessive battery cycling to "prove" renewable usage.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: 50–70 (polar-day months, PROVISIONAL range); NONE (polar-night months) UNIT: % of served load that is renewable TIME_SCOPE: Monthly rolling, seasonally differentiated APPLIES_TO: Whole-station energy balance EXCEPTIONS: Subordinate to reliability and reserve requirements at all times; not enforced during EMERGENCY modes. RATIONALE: A single flat renewable percentage target ignores that polar night makes solar contribution near zero for extended periods, and an overly aggressive target could tempt the EMS to under-charge batteries or curtail diesel reserve-building in favor of "renewable credit," which is operationally backwards. Measuring utilization-to-load (not generation) keeps the metric honest about what actually offsets diesel. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — actual solar/wind resource data, installed renewable capacity, and load profile are required to set a defensible target. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 10 — Renewable Curtailment Policy
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Curtailment is treated as an acceptable and sometimes necessary operational outcome — not something to be eliminated or heavily penalized — whenever it is required to protect battery health, respect generator minimum-output/stability constraints, or preserve emergency reserve margins. It should be minimized when it can be avoided at no cost to reliability or equipment protection, but is explicitly subordinate to reliability, safety, and equipment-protection constraints.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

Curtailment is allowed freely (no penalty) when: battery SOC is at or near its safe charge ceiling and no dispatchable load exists to absorb excess renewable output; the diesel generator is online and cannot be reduced below its technical minimum stable output (curtailing renewables instead of forcing an unsafe/inefficient generator turn-down or trip); forecast uncertainty means accepting more renewable now would risk overcharging batteries ahead of an anticipated high-generation period.
Curtailment should be minimized (soft preference, not hard rule) when: it results purely from a dispatch decision that could have been avoided by shifting deferrable load into the surplus window instead of curtailing generation.
Curtailment is bounded/reported, not banned: monthly curtailed-energy totals should be tracked and reported as a station-health/efficiency metric, but a nonzero curtailment rate is not, by itself, evidence of poor EMS performance.
Curtailment is explicitly secondary to reliability: the EMS must never avoid curtailment in a way that pushes battery SOC into an unsafe range or forces the generator outside safe operating limits.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: NONE (no fixed curtailment ceiling proposed; tracked as an informational metric) UNIT: kWh curtailed/month (tracking metric) TIME_SCOPE: Continuous dispatch decision; monthly reporting APPLIES_TO: Renewable generation subsystem (solar, wind) EXCEPTIONS: None — curtailment for battery/generator/reserve protection is always permitted regardless of operating mode. RATIONALE: Treating curtailment as inherently wasteful and penalizing it can push the EMS toward unsafe battery overcharging or forcing the generator into an inefficient/unsafe operating point just to "use" surplus renewable power — the opposite of good station management. Renewable energy that cannot be used safely is not a loss; the diesel and battery reserve it might have displaced is a separate, already-accounted-for cost. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — battery charge-rate limits, generator minimum stable output, and dispatchable-load inventory needed to fully specify when curtailment is unavoidable vs. avoidable. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 11 — Emergency Battery Reserve
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Define an explicit Emergency Battery Reserve (EBR) distinct from the technical minimum SOC (equipment protection floor) and from the forecast-uncertainty reserve margin (Decision 13). Proposed provisional EBR: 35% SOC, above the assumed ~20% technical minimum (itself unconfirmed here and subject to engineering validation), explicitly NOT simply adopting the current 20% technical-minimum figure as the emergency policy value.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

EBR SOC (PROVISIONAL): 35% state of charge, held as an operational reserve not to be drawn below during normal dispatch. This is separate from and above the technical minimum SOC that protects battery hardware from damage.
Varies by operating mode: In NORMAL_OPERATION and FORECAST_DEGRADED modes, the EMS should not plan dispatch that draws SOC below 35% except transiently. In STORM/EXTREME_WEATHER or DIESEL_CONSTRAINED modes, the EBR threshold may be temporarily raised (proposed +10 percentage points, i.e., 45%, PROVISIONAL) as a precautionary measure ahead of anticipated stress. In a declared BATTERY_EMERGENCY or CRITICAL_INFRASTRUCTURE_EMERGENCY, the EBR may be violated (drawn below 35%, down to the technical minimum) but never below the technical minimum SOC.
What constitutes an emergency permitting EBR violation: Loss of diesel generation capability (mechanical failure, fuel exhaustion) combined with insufficient renewable input to meet CRITICAL load; or a declared life-safety emergency requiring maximum available power regardless of long-term battery health.
Restoration after emergency: Once an emergency ends, the EMS should prioritize restoring SOC back above the EBR threshold as soon as safely possible (via diesel and/or renewable charging), and this restoration period should itself be tracked and reported.
Interaction with forecast uncertainty: Forecast uncertainty does not change the EBR itself but may add an additional, separately tracked reserve margin on top of it (see Decision 13) — the two must not be merged into a single unexplained number.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: 35 (normal EBR, PROVISIONAL); 45 (elevated EBR in precautionary modes, PROVISIONAL) UNIT: % state of charge TIME_SCOPE: Continuous; elevated threshold active during precautionary mode declarations APPLIES_TO: Station battery bank EXCEPTIONS: May be drawn below only in declared BATTERY_EMERGENCY / CRITICAL_INFRASTRUCTURE_EMERGENCY, never below the technical minimum SOC (hardware protection floor). RATIONALE: Conflating "the battery might be damaged below this point" with "we have no safety margin left for an emergency" produces a dangerously thin buffer — if the EMS is willing to run the battery down to the hardware-damage floor during ordinary operation, there is no reserve left when an actual emergency (generator failure, extreme storm) hits. A separate, higher EBR gives the station genuine emergency headroom. STATION_SPECIFIC_DATA_REQUIRED: YES — actual battery chemistry, degradation curve, and true technical minimum SOC must be confirmed by engineering; 35%/45% are provisional stakeholder starting points, not measured values. ENGINEERING_VALIDATION_REQUIRED: YES FINAL_AUTHORITY_REQUIRED: YES

DECISION 12 — Terminal SOC Requirement
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: The EMS optimization horizon should end with battery SOC at or above the Emergency Battery Reserve threshold (proposed 35%, per Decision 11) under normal conditions, with a higher terminal target during periods of anticipated low renewable input (storm forecast, onset of polar night) and a defined minimum floor that is never violated by design at the horizon boundary.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

Minimum terminal SOC (normal/expected-renewable conditions): 35% (equal to EBR), so that the station always ends an optimization horizon with at least its emergency buffer intact rather than planning to "spend down to nothing and hope the next forecast is accurate."
Target terminal SOC (storm preparation / forecast-degraded / approaching polar night): 55–65% (PROVISIONAL), reflecting a precautionary buffer against the next horizon's renewable input being lower than expected.
Operating-mode exceptions: During a declared BATTERY_EMERGENCY, the terminal SOC requirement is relaxed to "as high as achievable given the emergency," since forcing a terminal SOC target during an active emergency could itself cause unnecessary load shedding.
Rationale for not copying current optimizer behavior: The current implementation's terminal SOC behavior is a downstream consequence of its penalty coefficients, not a deliberately chosen stakeholder value; it should not be assumed correct simply because it is what the software currently does.
NUMERICAL_VALUE: 35 (minimum, normal conditions, PROVISIONAL); 55–65 (target, precautionary conditions, PROVISIONAL) UNIT: % state of charge TIME_SCOPE: End of each EMS optimization/dispatch horizon (e.g., end of rolling 24–48 hour window, actual horizon length TBD by engineering) APPLIES_TO: Station battery bank EXCEPTIONS: Relaxed during declared BATTERY_EMERGENCY. RATIONALE: Ending every planning horizon at a low SOC "gambles" that the next period's forecast will be accurate and favorable; ending consistently at a healthy SOC preserves flexibility to handle a bad-weather surprise, overnight demand, or a delayed diesel start. This is precautionary and mirrors real polar-station practice of never letting reserves run to the edge as a matter of routine planning. PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: Adopt a conservative deterministic reserve-margin approach as the baseline policy, with a receding-horizon (rolling re-optimization) correction mechanism, rather than full stochastic/scenario optimization — on the grounds that a simple, explainable margin-based approach is more operationally enforceable and auditable for station staff than a probabilistic formulation, while still being meaningfully protective. A move to scenario/stochastic optimization may be a reasonable future engineering enhancement but is not proposed as the initial stakeholder policy.

POLICY_DEFINITION:

Solar uncertainty: Apply a conservative downward adjustment to forecast solar generation when planning dispatch — proposed provisional derate of 15–25% below the point forecast during shoulder seasons (rapid day-length change, higher cloud variability), and treat solar as unavailable (0% credit) during polar night. Value PROVISIONAL, pending validation against actual forecast-vs-actual solar performance data.
Wind uncertainty: Apply a conservative derate to forecast wind generation, proposed provisional 20–30% downward adjustment, wider than solar because wind forecasts are typically less reliable at sub-day resolution, especially ahead of storm fronts. PROVISIONAL.
Demand uncertainty: Apply a conservative upward adjustment to forecast demand, proposed provisional +10% above point-forecast load, to account for unplanned scientific equipment use, emergency heating needs, or personnel changes. PROVISIONAL.

DECISION 15 — Objective Priority Order
PROPOSED_STAKEHOLDER_POSITION: The following priority order is proposed, developed from the station-manager perspective rather than adopting the example ordering verbatim:

Human and station safety (hard constraint — never traded off)
Critical-load reliability (near-hard constraint; see Decision 06)
Battery and generator equipment protection (hard constraint — technical minimum SOC, safe generator operating range; protecting the equipment that makes future reliability possible)
Emergency resilience / reserve maintenance (EBR, terminal SOC — protects the station's ability to handle the next emergency, not just the current moment)
Diesel fuel conservation (liters consumed, within the fuel budget)
Renewable utilization (secondary efficiency objective)
Operating cost / generator wear (runtime, starts) (informational/secondary; matters for logistics but not safety-relevant)
Noncritical/comfort-load service and mission convenience optimization (lowest priority; shed first, optimized last)
POLICY_DEFINITION:

Primary objective: Ensure human safety and CRITICAL-load reliability under all foreseeable conditions (items 1–2).
Secondary objectives: Protect the equipment (battery, generator) that reliability structurally depends on, and maintain emergency reserves so the station is never one bad forecast away from crisis (items 3–4).
Tertiary objectives: Minimize diesel consumption and maximize sensible renewable utilization, in that order — fuel logistics risk is treated as more operationally significant than renewable-percentage optics, given a remote station's resupply difficulty (items 5–6).
Lower priority: Cost and equipment-wear minimization and noncritical/comfort-load service (items 7–8) — real, but never allowed to influence decisions 1–4.
Hard constraints: Items 1, 2 (except in declared emergency, where 2 shifts to "minimize unmet CRITICAL load" per Decision 06), and 3.
Emergency overrides: In a declared emergency, items 5–8 are immediately and explicitly subordinated; the EMS should not "average" fuel/renewable/cost considerations against safety in an emergency — safety wins outright, then reliability, then equipment protection, in that strict order, for the duration of the declared emergency.
Why this differs from the example ordering given in the task: Equipment protection (battery/generator) is elevated above emergency-resilience-reserve and fuel logistics in this proposal, because damaging the battery or generator during a "normal" attempt to squeeze out extra reliability or fuel savings would undermine every objective below it for the rest of the season — protecting the equipment is what makes the reliability and resilience objectives achievable at all, not a competing objective with them.

NUMERICAL_VALUE: NONE (ordering, not a numeric policy) UNIT: NONE TIME_SCOPE: Continuous; re-asserted explicitly under each operating mode APPLIES_TO: Entire EMS objective structure EXCEPTIONS: Emergency override sequence as described. RATIONALE: A station manager cannot treat "minimize cost" and "keep people safe" as numbers on the same scale; a strict priority ordering, with explicit hard constraints at the top and clearly subordinate objectives below, is what real station operations already implicitly follow and is more defensible to safety officers, funders, and regulators than a single blended objective function.
