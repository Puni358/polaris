# Current Error Diagnosis 

The remaining ~28% nMAE error appears dominated by *cloud forecast limitations* and high-latitude effects rather than missing tabular features.  High-latitude irradiance error is largely driven by **uncertainties in cloud optical properties and geometry**, not captured by standard NWP outputs.  In practice, NWP models struggle to predict the exact cloud cover, thickness, and three-dimensional structure around the station (hypotheses A–C above).  For example, Riise et al. (2024) identify cloud optical depth as the primary driver of irradiance uncertainty at northern sites, with additional error from surface albedo (snow) and broken/cloud-edge effects.  Likewise, operational high-latitude studies find that geostationary methods outperform NWP only for the first 2–3 hours, after which even a basic NWP forecast is more accurate.  This implies that (A) **cloud-timing and motion errors** in NWP (and the inability to resolve small-scale cloud fields) are a leading cause of error.  High solar zenith angles (low sun) and horizon shading (F) also introduce nonlinear error, as clouds and snow reflectance become more sensitive to geometry.  

In contrast, most “plateau” features (e.g. adding winds, ensemble spread, terrain/horizon, etc.) have only marginal effects, suggesting that **intrinsic predictability limits** (dominantly cloud variability) are now controlling performance.  In summary, the most plausible dominant error sources (ranked) are: (1) *NWP cloud field errors* (timing, coverage, opacity) and associated radiative-transfer assumptions; (2) *low-sun/high-SZA effects* including horizon shading and snow-albedo (affecting beam/diffuse ratio); (3) *spatial heterogeneity and cloud-edge effects* (unresolved sub-grid variability); and (4) *model architecture/temporal context limits* (e.g. static regression cannot correct dynamic cloud evolution).  Lesser roles likely come from (E) direct-vs-diffuse decomposition and (I) regime transitions, which are symptoms of the above.  In short, the plateau points to *missing information* about evolving cloud cover rather than missing static predictors.  

# Top 10 Candidate Approaches 

- **Satellite-image cloud nowcasting (Deep CNN)**.  Use sequences of geostationary satellite images (e.g. Meteosat HRV or SEVIRI) with a convolutional network (e.g. U-Net) to predict future cloud cover indices and then derive GHI (à la HelioNet).  This directly supplies detailed cloud motion/timing information absent from NWP.  Straub et al. (2024) show that a CNN forecasting visible-range cloud index can cut irradiance RMSE by ~8–15% relative to traditional cloud-motion-vector methods.  (This addresses hypotheses A–B by learning actual cloud evolution.)  

- **All-sky camera image forecasting**.  If a ground-based sky imager is available at Nuolja, a deep model can forecast the sky scene a few minutes ahead to capture very short-term cloud changes.  Sky-imaging methods have yielded strong improvements for horizons up to ~10–30 min.  (No direct citation above, but see e.g. MDPI studies on ASI-based nowcasting.)  This would supply cloud advection data at the exact location, directly tackling cloud-motion error.  

- **Cloud-optical-depth + radiative-transfer hybrid**.  Forecast cloud optical thickness (COD) explicitly and feed it into a physics-based radiative model.  For example, Peng et al. (2026) use satellite COD and cloud mask forecasts as inputs to an all-sky irradiance model, yielding more physically consistent GHI predictions.  This hybrid approach (ML for COD, then clear-sky/RT for GHI) enforces known physics (beam/diffuse separation and attenuation) and improves interpretability.  It directly targets hypothesis D and F by modeling how clouds attenuate radiation.  

- **Direct/Diffuse multitask learning**.  Train a multitask model to predict GHI, DNI, and DHI jointly.  By separating direct-beam vs diffuse irradiance, the model can learn distinct dependencies (e.g. high SZA effects mainly alter diffuse fraction).  This representation may help capture nonlinear sun-angle effects (F) that a single GHI target would average out.  (Although no major benchmark cited, multitask approaches are common in PV forecasting.)

- **Broken-cloud/regime mixture-of-experts**.  Build a regime classifier (e.g. clear, overcast, broken) and train specialized models per regime, or use a learned mixture-of-experts that gates by cloudiness.  The idea is that different physical regimes have different error characteristics, and a “one-size-fits-all” model dilutes performance.  Relatedly, Erniyazov et al. (2026) use a mixture-of-experts head and achieve up to 15% RMSE reduction over baseline deep models.  A regime model could better capture the discontinuities in the GHI response when clouds appear/disappear (hypotheses I).  

- **Spatiotemporal deep sequence model**.  Instead of a static regression, use a sequence model (e.g. ConvLSTM or Transformer) on the time series of input features (past NWP forecasts, past irradiance, etc.).  Convolutional LSTM networks have proven highly effective for spatiotemporal weather nowcasting (e.g. precipitation nowcast up to 2 h).  A deep temporal model could learn evolving patterns (e.g. evolving cloud cover or diurnal trends) that a static XGBoost cannot.  This addresses hypothesis H by leveraging memory of recent conditions.  

- **Spatial analog or neighbor fusion (GNN)**.  Incorporate information from nearby stations or grid points using a Graph Neural Network.  For example, Erniyazov et al. fuse NWP fields across locations with a GNN and dramatically improve forecasts.  For a single site, one could instead use multiple nearby grid columns as “neighbors” (or historical analog days) to capture spatial context (hypotheses C).  

- **Differentiable radiative-transfer models**.  Embed a simple radiative transfer layer (e.g. a learned cloud transmittance model) inside the ML pipeline.  For instance, train a neural submodule to estimate atmospheric transmittance given NWP cloud water, so that the final irradiance prediction is physically constrained.  This could reduce violations of clear-sky physics and improve generalization.  (No direct citation, but related to physics-informed NN ideas.)  

- **Analog-based or weather-regime ensembles**.  Use nearest-neighbor matching of current atmospheric conditions to historical cases and derive forecasts from analog precedents.  For example, find past MEPS states similar to the current MEPS and use their observed GHI as a forecast, or use ensemble analogs.  Analog methods can capture rare patterns (broken clouds, transitions) missed by regression.  (Such approaches are well-known in analog forecasting, though we lack a specific solar citation.)  

- **Refined clear-sky baseline modeling**.  Improve the clear-sky model and forecast of the clear-sky index.  For example, use a high-precision sun-position and aerosol model (e.g. incorporate measured AOD or satellite aerosol), so that the model only needs to learn cloud departures.  This could reduce baseline bias and focus learning on cloud error.  (This approach is partly done via k_t target and is in spirit of hybrid models.)  

# Top 3 Breakthrough Candidates 

**1. Satellite-image-based cloud nowcasting (HelioNet-style CNN).** *Mechanism:* Uses real-time satellite images to directly track cloud cover and project its movement, capturing small-scale cloud motion and evolution that NWP misses.  This adds truly new information (observed cloud fields) instead of relying on model forecasts. *Existing gap:* Current POLARIS features only include NWP and derived variables; no continuous cloud imagery.  An ML model like HelioNet learns spatiotemporal cloud patterns that static features cannot represent. *Expected upside:* Straub et al. report >8% RMSE reduction in GHI by CNN forecasting of cloud-index images.  If similar gains apply here, this could cut error by a few percent nMAE. *Evidence:* HelioNet achieved an ~8–15% RMSE improvement over cloud-motion-vector baseline. Satellite nowcasting is state-of-the-art for short horizons (HelioNet for 0–4h; similar success in Finland). *Data required:* Geostationary cloud imagery (visible/infrared) at ~3–5 km resolution, 5–15 min cadence (e.g. SEVIRI or future Meteosat), plus clear-sky radiance model. Historical archives (~2020–24) exist from EUMETSAT. *Causal availability:* Yes – imagery at t₀ is immediately available for short-term forecast. Nighttime/polar twilight can be masked or ignored. *Implementation:* Train a CNN (e.g. U-Net) to forecast future cloud-index maps from past imagery, then apply Heliosat to get GHI.  Requires handling polar geometry and possibly attenuating effects. Complexity is high (deep learning on large images, GPU needed), but feasible. *Risk:* At high latitude, geostationary viewing angle is oblique and sun is low, so cloud detection can be noisy; twilight masking needed. Also, only aids short horizons (likely <6 h), so benefit beyond current horizon must be evaluated. *Probability >1% nMAE gain:* Moderate–high, given published 8% RMSE gains. *Probability >5% gain:* Modest; HelioNet’s ~8% RMSE reduction suggests possibly ~1–2% nMAE improvement. This is one of the most promising novel sources of predictive information.  

**2. Cloud-optical-depth + radiative-transfer model.** *Mechanism:* Predict the actual cloud optical thickness (COD) and then use a clear-sky transmittance formula to compute GHI.  This hybrid embeds physical law: e.g. Lambert-Beer attenuation given COD and solar geometry. *Existing gap:* POLARIS currently predicts GHI directly from variables; it doesn’t explicitly model how clouds attenuate the sun. Any nonlinear radiative effects (multiple scattering, cloud side illumination) are only implicit in ML. *Expected upside:* By splitting the problem, we could reduce model “burden”.  Peng et al. (2026) show that forecasting COD and feeding it to a radiative model yields reliable 10-min–4h forecasts, with RMSE ~100–150 W/m². In theory, this could reduce bias under diffuse/cloudy conditions by enforcing physics. *Evidence:* Peng et al.’s hybrid ML-physics method explicitly handled aerosol, water vapor, albedo, and COD to improve interpretability. While direct accuracy gains on nMAE aren’t reported, the method reduced bias significantly. A similar concept (CamS or “all-sky model”) underlies many irradiance models, implying high potential. *Data required:* Satellite-derived cloud mask and COD (e.g. from EUMETSAT or NOAA polar). If geostationary COD is not available at this latitude, one could learn an ML mapper from NWP cloud water to COD. Also needs aerosol optical depth, water vapor column, surface albedo, and real-time sun angle (all either measured or from NWP).  Historical data may be patchy but some proxies exist (e.g. CAMS aerosol and climatology). *Causal availability:* Satellite cloud mask is typically available with <1h latency; CAMS or similar aerosol data is near-real-time. Radiative calculations are instantaneous. *Implementation:* Build an ML model that forecasts COD (and cloud mask) given current satellite/NWP data, then plug into a radiative transfer code (e.g. libRadtran or simplified all-sky models) to get GHI.  Complexity is moderate: two-stage modeling with a radiative core. *Risk:* Accuracy depends on quality of COD retrievals and the radiative model. At high latitudes, cloud-crater 3D effects and sun angle may violate simple assumptions. If COD forecasts are poor, performance suffers. *Probability >1% gain:* Plausible, if clouds often misrepresented. It might correct systematic bias (especially in winter or snow). *Probability >5% gain:* Uncertain – likely smaller than pure new-info methods. This is promising for interpretability and moderate error reduction but may not fully break the plateau alone.  

**3. Spatiotemporal deep learning (ConvLSTM/Transformer).** *Mechanism:* Replace the static regression model with a deep sequence model that ingests a history of feature vectors (past hours of NWP forecasts, past irradiance, etc.) and learns temporal patterns.  E.g. a Transformer or ConvLSTM can attend to evolving cloud proxies and adapt predictions based on recent trends. *Existing gap:* Current POLARIS stages appear to predict each lead time independently (no reported use of past target or cloud history as features beyond one-step corrections). This ignores any continuity in cloud cover or diurnal trends beyond fixed features. *Expected upside:* Sequence models can capture inertia and temporal correlations. For example, convolutional LSTM models have achieved strong performance in nowcasting precipitation up to 2 h by learning motion patterns, and Transformers have revolutionized time-series forecasting. For irradiance, this could help under partially cloudy, rapidly changing conditions by “remembering” the immediate past. *Evidence:* While no direct solar cite here, the general success of ConvLSTM and Transformer models in weather suggests significant potential. Erniyazov et al. (2026) showed attention-based fusion beating standard models by ~10–15%. Also, the error plateau hints that vanilla trees have limited capacity for temporal dependencies. *Data required:* A time series of past POLARIS input vectors (NWP features, past GHI) for each prediction. No new external data is required beyond what’s currently used. *Causal availability:* Past data up to now is trivially causal. *Implementation:* Train a recurrent or transformer network on past T hours of data to predict future irradiance.  Complexity is high (neural network sequence learning), and careful training needed to avoid overfitting. *Risk:* Gains may be incremental if the dominant error is unpredictable cloud evolution (i.e. memory only helps so much). Also, deep sequence models can overfit small datasets. *Probability >1% gain:* Moderate; could address regime persistence (ramping clouds) that current model misses. *Probability >5% gain:* Lower; likely limited improvement unless very smart architecture is used. Nonetheless, this directly targets the lack of temporal context (hypothesis H).  

# One Best Bet 

**Satellite-image-based cloud nowcasting** is the single most promising approach for a meaningful jump in accuracy.  Unlike feature additions, it injects *new information* (actual cloud imagery) that directly reduces NWP uncertainty.  The cited HelioNet study demonstrates that a deep CNN on satellite cloud-index images can significantly outperform conventional methods.  Given Nuolja’s location, high-resolution visible/infrared images (Meteosat HRV/SEVIRI) exist for daylight hours, providing near-causal snapshots of cloud cover. A well-designed spatiotemporal CNN or ConvLSTM can exploit this to predict cloud cover minutes to hours ahead, effectively replacing part of the NWP cloud forecast. This tackles the dominant error sources (cloud timing and 3D structure) head-on. In contrast, purely tabular ML has likely exhausted most easy gains.  We therefore regard satellite-CNN forecasting as the highest-reward bet.  It “moves the needle” by ~5–10% error reduction in analogous studies, and it addresses multiple hypotheses (A, B, C, D) in one model.  

# Experiment Design 

**Testbed:** Collect archived geostationary satellite images (visible channel) covering Nuolja for at least 2 years (e.g. Meteosat HRV, 15-min cadence).  Also assemble the matching ground GHI observations. **Approach:** Precompute a cloud-index (CI) or cloud mask from each image (Heliosat or simple threshold) to serve as target images. Train a convolutional U-Net (similar to HelioNet) that takes the past *k* CI images (e.g. last 4 × 15 min) and predicts the next *m* CI images (up to ~4 h). Bias-correct and feed predicted CI into the Heliosat irradiance model to get GHI. **Evaluation:** Compare this ML-based satellite nowcast to a baseline (current POLARIS model and to satellite persistence/optical-flow).  Use held-out year (2025 data) as “ODD” test.  Metrics: hourly nMAE/RMSE of GHI.  Carefully check causality (use only imagery available up to forecast time). No hyperparameter tuning beyond this prototype. **Causality:** Meteosat images are real-time available; using only past imagery (no peeking ahead) makes the test fully causal. **Feasibility:** This experiment requires no changes to existing POLARIS pipeline beyond data ingestion; it is self-contained. **Success criterion:** Achieving a statistically significant reduction in nMAE (e.g. >1 percentage point) over the frozen champion would support the approach’s viability. 

# What **Not** to Do 

- **Do not rely solely on more tabular NWP features or static corrections.** The plateau suggests these have been mined thoroughly with little gain.  Adding yet another pressure level or cloud parameter (without new information) is unlikely to break through.  
- **Avoid generic end-to-end DL on the same inputs.**  Simply replacing XGBoost with a big neural net on the same data usually yields marginal changes (no new causal info).  
- **Skip blind “hyperparameter hunts.”**  Hyperparameter or architecture tweaks (e.g. more trees, deeper nets) without new data sources will likely overfit and give only tiny CV lifts.  
- **Don’t add nonspecific proxies.** For example, installing a LiDAR or scanning cloud radar at Nuolja might not be feasible or causal for wider use. Similarly, relying on infrequent polar-orbit satellite passes adds latency.  
- **Be wary of overly complex multi-station solutions.** Ideas like GNNs with a broad network of stations (as in Erniyazov2026) sound attractive, but Nuolja is isolated in a mountain. Without many near sensors, a huge GNN may not generalize here. 
- **Avoid heavy trust in pure NWP post-processing.** Treating the NWP forecast as nearly perfect and just “smoothing” it with a model (e.g. Kalman filtering) has been tried (e.g. GBDT residual correction) with diminishing returns; the remaining error is largely unpredictable.  

# Target Realism 

Achieving **<20% nMAE** is plausible *in principle*, but requires significant new capability (e.g. combining satellite nowcasting and physics).  A sub-20% result would likely need multiple novel components working together. Reaching **<15%** nMAE seems **unlikely in the short term** – it would demand almost perfect cloud tracking or an unexpectedly powerful new approach.  Hitting **~10%** nMAE appears *extremely ambitious*, given the chaotic nature of clouds, especially at high latitudes. In reality, irreducible uncertainty (small cumulus, measurement noise, extreme low sun, etc.) likely sets a floor above 10%. We should aim for incremental breakthroughs (e.g. a few percent improvement) and remain skeptical of dramatic claims. 

**Sources:** Contemporary solar-forecast literature and high-latitude irradiance studies support these conclusions.  For example, expert benchmarks show NWP rivals or beats satellite forecasts after a few hours, and error analyses highlight cloud optical depth as the top uncertainty.  Advanced ML/Solar works demonstrate that satellite-cloud CNNs can materially cut error and that multi-source deep models dramatically outperform simple models. These guide the above recommendations.
