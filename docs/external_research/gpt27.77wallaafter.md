# Adversarial Review of Out-of-Model Information Sources

## 1. AROME-Arctic / Independent Arctic NWP  
- **Novelty (Category A):** AROME-Arctic is a high-resolution regional NWP model for the European Arctic (2.5 km grid) that is *not* part of the standard global or Nordic ensemble (MEPS) currently used. It has been in service since 2015 and forecasts unique Arctic phenomena (polar lows, sea-ice edge processes) that global models struggle with. This is genuinely new information relative to existing MEPS/ECMWF inputs.  
- **Causality:** AROME-Arctic explicitly models processes unique to high latitudes (e.g. polar clouds, surface fluxes over ice). Incorporating its forecasts could causally improve prediction of Arctic weather. However, if the POLARIS target domain is mostly mid-latitude, its relevance is limited to northern areas.  
- **2024 Coverage:** Historical AROME-Arctic analyses/forecasts are archived (MET Norway provides 0–60 h output). Thus 2024 data exist for retrospective use.  
- **Spatial Representativeness:** Covers Svalbard, northern Norway and adjacent seas. Very good for far north, but provides no data for lower latitudes or southern regions – a spatial gap outside the Arctic.  
- **H1–H24 Usefulness:** Designed for 0–60 h, so it directly covers the 1–24 h range. It can capture small-scale convective/arctic phenomena that global models miss.  
- **Failure Modes:** Outside its domain, AROME forecasts are invalid (it has a limited Arctic domain). Over sea ice, even AROME is uncertain (sparse obs). It may have bias (e.g. cold bias over ice) or not assimilate tropical information. It also does not help if the forecast error stems from mid-latitude large-scale dynamics.  
- **Expected Upside:** Likely modest. It could boost skill for Arctic station forecasts and polar precipitation, but if the champion already handles mid-latitude weather well, adding AROME may yield only incremental gains in some northern areas.  
- **Feasibility:** Technically feasible (data available on open servers), but integration into the locked POLARIS system is nontrivial (requires ingesting a new high-res model). If the model code cannot be retrained, simply swapping in AROME fields as features is likely impossible without re-design.  
- **Evidence:** MET Norway describes AROME-Arctic as “a high‐resolution model [2.5 km] that forecasts the weather in the High North for the next three days”. It covers polar lows and sea-ice-edge processes unique to Arctic weather. Thus AROME-Arctic is new and potentially informative, but its added value outside the Arctic is limited and integration may violate the locked-benchmark constraint.

## 2. Satellite Cloud Optical Fields  
- **Novelty (Category A):** Using *optical* (visible/near-IR) cloud products (e.g. cloud optical thickness, effective radius) is a new data source not already in MEPS/NWP. Champion models typically use NWP variables or IR radiances, so visible-cloud metrics would be genuinely new daytime information.  
- **Causality:** Visible channel measurements provide high-resolution cloud morphology and optical properties. Studies show that combinations of cloud optical thickness (τ) and effective drop radius correlate strongly with rainfall: large τ and sufficient droplet size indicate high rain probability. In other words, cloud optical fields have a direct physical link to precipitation likelihood.  
- **2024 Coverage:** Geostationary satellites (e.g. Meteosat, Himawari, GOES) provide continuous visible imagery during daylight. Products like cloud optical thickness can be derived or are available from remote-sensing services. Daytime 2024 data are available globally; some polar-orbiter retrievals exist for night but are sparse.  
- **Spatial Representativeness:** Visible data are global (geostationary covers mid-latitudes well, polar orbiters cover the poles). But there is zero coverage at night except from polar-orbiters (and only at IR wavelengths). So representativeness is strictly daytime.  
- **H1–H24 Usefulness:** Optical fields are “snapshot” current-state data – great for nowcasting (H1–H6) and possibly informing short-horizon trends (cloud advection). They do not themselves predict future change beyond identifying current cloud masses. A model could extrapolate observed cloud movement or evolution, but raw optical fields mainly aid immediate forecast.  
- **Failure Modes:** No night-time data (visible retrievals cease at sunset), so forecasts for night hours get no new optical info. Also, many precipitation systems (e.g. frontal stratiform rain) may have subtle optical signatures that are hard to interpret. The champion might misinterpret thick stratocumulus vs convective clouds. Without careful use, adding optical features may add noise.  
- **Expected Upside:** Moderate for daytime convective events. The evidence from Nauss et al. (2006) shows that optical cloud properties can distinguish raining vs non-raining clouds: “the probability distribution clearly corroborates … that rainfall is connected to large enough combinations” of optical thickness and droplet radius. Thus, in principle, optical fields could reduce daytime error by better identifying convective rain. Overall improvement may be limited by day-only coverage.  
- **Feasibility:** Data are available via satellite archives, but incorporating them likely requires new data pipelines or re-training, which is prohibited. Also, the locked model cannot ingest raw satellite images directly. In practice this route probably cannot be implemented without major changes.  
- **Evidence:** Satellite visible channels are known to give “very high-resolution information on cloud structure and optical properties”, but are only usable in daytime. Research confirms cloud optical depth combined with microphysical parameters delineates precipitation. This suggests real information content, but practical constraints (nighttime gap, locked model) severely limit feasibility.

## 3. Satellite Cloud Motion / Cloud Evolution  
- **Novelty (Category A):** Using explicit cloud motion vectors (CMVs) from satellite imagery is new to the champion if it currently relies only on NWP. CMVs are not a feature in raw MEPS output; they would be an additional information layer derived from geostationary imagery.  
- **Causality:** CMVs (and related atmospheric motion vectors) represent observed cloud/water-vapor advection. By tracking clouds over ~15-minute intervals, one can infer the wind and project cloud positions forward. This purely observational extrapolation is physically meaningful for short-term forecasts: “displacement vectors” assume features propagate at constant velocity and are fundamental to nowcasting. However, it assumes little change in cloud intensity.  
- **2024 Coverage:** Geostationary satellites (Meteosat MSG, Himawari, GOES) continuously deliver IR images every 5–15 min. Many agencies already compute CMV products in real time. Historical data are widely archived (e.g. EUMETSAT). Coverage is global for mid-latitudes; polar coverage is limited.  
- **Spatial Representativeness:** Covers large areas seen by geostationary sats (roughly 60°S–60°N, some polar). Each CMV has a location and height (inferred from IR brightness). But CMVs only exist where there are clouds to track, and they sample mostly the flow where clouds exist (updraft regions). So the dataset is biased toward regions of convection or stratiform cloud.  
- **H1–H24 Usefulness:** CMVs are excellent for very short-range (nowcasting, ~0–3 h) extrapolation of existing cloud systems. Their reliability decays beyond a few hours as clouds evolve (grow/decay) or new convection develops. Therefore, their added value for 1–3 h may be tangible, but near-zero by 24 h.  
- **Failure Modes:** Since clouds evolve, CMV-based forecasts can fail if systems intensify or dissipate (they assume persistence). Dense cloud fields or overlapping cloud layers can confuse tracking. CMVs only capture cloud-top motion – e.g. “cloud winds… reflect only one level and only updraft areas”. They cannot predict storm initiation or dissipation.  
- **Expected Upside:** Limited to very short lead times. For H1–H3, a well-executed CMV extrapolation can provide a realistic shift of cloud/precipitation patterns. But beyond that, the rigid-advection assumption breaks down. The expected error reduction in H1–H3 may be small if the champion already has a good NWP, and negligible by 6–24 h.  
- **Feasibility:** Moderate. CMVs are standard operational products (EUMETSAT), but injecting them into a locked ML model would require architectural support (the model needs to accept motion fields or successive images). Without re-training, one could not easily use them. Thus under the locked benchmark this is nearly impossible to implement in practice.  
- **Evidence:** EUMeTrain notes that CMVs “are calculated by…joining successive positions of an identifiable cloud” from IR images and are “a useful source of information for nowcasting”. However, they caution that “cloud winds represent a limited and meteorologically biased data set” (only cloud-top, convective regions). In summary, cloud motion is real information, but only for very short-term forecasts, and it cannot overcome fundamental limits or locked-model constraints.

## 4. Multi-Modal Satellite + NWP Fusion  
- **Novelty (Category A/C):** Combining satellite observations (visible/IR radiances or products) with NWP data is conceptually new relative to a purely NWP-based model. Modern ML weather models (e.g. FuXi Weather) indeed fuse multi-satellite data with forecasting. This could be labeled A (new data source) *and* C (requires new model architecture to fuse data).  
- **Causality:** Direct assimilation of satellite observations provides updated initial conditions. FuXi Weather, for example, shows that assimilating multi-channel satellite data “extends skillful forecast lead times beyond ECMWF HRES”. In theory, adding observed cloud/temperature profiles tightens the fit to reality and improves forecasts.  
- **2024 Coverage:** All relevant satellite channels (microwave sounders, IR, visible) are available and archived. The FuXi system used polar-orbiting microwave sounders and others. So data exist for retrospective assimilation.  
- **Spatial Representativeness:** Global (polar-orbiters + geostationary) with some gaps. Cloud-top visible is day-only, but IR/microwave provide day-night. Most of Earth is covered by some sat. FuXi notes “orbiting satellites contribute ~85% of data assimilated into global NWP”, implying good global reach.  
- **H1–H24 Usefulness:** Assimilating satellite data primarily affects initial conditions, which can improve forecasts at all lead times, especially short to medium range. It does not directly predict weather, but by starting from a better state. Studies (like FuXi) show improvements even out to 10 days. For 1–24 h, the effect is strong because the model starts closer to reality.  
- **Likely Failure Modes:** Implementing true data assimilation in a locked ML pipeline is effectively impossible. Satellite data are indirect (radiances), requiring observation operators and possibly re-training the model to ingest them. If done naively, sensor biases or cloud parameterization errors can introduce noise. FuXi achieved gains by complex training – not available here.  
- **Expected Upside:** Potentially large (as shown by FuXi’s success), **if** full assimilation is done. But under the locked model, one could perhaps only feed some satellite-derived features (cloud cover, brightness temp). The locked setup likely means zero gain unless model retraining is allowed.  
- **Feasibility:** Essentially **impossible** under locked constraints. Multi-modal fusion needs retraining or retraining-like assimilation pipelines, which is prohibited. Thus this route cannot be implemented in practice without major revision of the forecast system.  
- **Evidence:** The new ML system “FuXi Weather” explicitly *assimilates data from multiple satellites* and outperforms ECMWF’s own forecasts, demonstrating the power of satellite+NWP fusion. However, this required a novel ML architecture and training; by itself this suggests that simply adding satellite inputs is not a plug-and-play fix under a locked model. 

## 5. Joint H1–H24 Sequence Forecasting  
- **Novelty (Category C):** This is an **architectural change**: training a model to output a 24-hour forecast sequence jointly, instead of independent one-hour forecasts. It does not introduce new external data, only a new modeling approach. Thus it is **not** an “out-of-model” information source, but a method innovation.  
- **Causality:** In principle, enforcing temporal coherence could exploit autocorrelations (e.g. a storm’s evolution over time). However, weather dynamics are complex and often require explicit physical modeling; simply predicting 24 hours as a sequence from static inputs may not capture time-varying processes better than stepwise forecasting. There is no new causal input – it’s using the same data in a different way.  
- **2024 Coverage:** Not applicable (no new external data).  
- **Spatial Representativeness:** Not applicable.  
- **H1–H24 Usefulness:** Potentially modest. Joint forecasting might reduce inconsistencies between hours and could slightly improve accuracy by sharing information across leads. But it cannot overcome the fundamental uncertainty growth with lead time. Indeed, if implemented badly it could propagate errors across the entire sequence.  
- **Failure Modes:** A single model generating 24h can suffer from error accumulation and mode collapse (predicting average weather). Without additional information, extending lead time will magnify error. It also violates the locked-model rule: the champion’s model cannot be retrained into a new sequence-output architecture.  
- **Expected Upside:** Probably small. Some ML systems use sequence models for precipitation nowcasting (ConvLSTM), but they typically handle very short horizons (few hours) and still degrade quickly. For a full H1–24 day forecast, the gains over independent forecasts are likely marginal.  
- **Feasibility:** **Impossible** under the locked benchmark. This change would require retraining or replacing the model with a new RNN/sequence architecture. Since no model training is allowed, we cannot implement this approach at all.  
- **Evidence:** General time-series forecasting literature suggests that multi-step forecasting can give consistent trajectories, but suffers from accumulating error (and is not equivalent to new data). No specific POLARIS evidence is available, and given locked constraints, this remains purely a theoretical improvement. 

## 6. Probabilistic Forecasting  
- **Novelty (Category C):** This is also an **architectural/methodological change**, not new external data. It means issuing ensembles or predictive distributions instead of a single deterministic forecast. If the champion currently outputs a point forecast (27.7% nMAE), switching to probabilistic output would not introduce any new physics or data.  
- **Causality:** Representing uncertainty does not change the forecast mean or error; it only provides additional calibration or risk information. It may improve decision-making, but it cannot directly reduce the deterministic error metric. There is no additional causal information – it’s simply reinterpreting existing model outputs.  
- **2024 Coverage:** N/A.  
- **Spatial Representativeness:** N/A.  
- **H1–H24 Usefulness:** Probabilistic forecasts can improve reliability (e.g. better capture of event probabilities), but they do not improve mean-squared or absolute error. For the given nMAE metric, a probabilistic forecast ensemble might allow a better multi-model mean but we already have one.  
- **Failure Modes:** If only meant as an output technique, none. But since no extra data are introduced, it will not “break the error bottleneck.” Under locked constraints, one cannot simply add uncertainty output.  
- **Expected Upside:** Essentially zero improvement on the current error metric. It could offer risk info, but cannot reduce the nMAE on ODD data by itself.  
- **Feasibility:** Technically easy (produce ensemble or quantile output), but futile. Under the letter of “out-of-model information,” this is irrelevant – it’s an internal model change.  
- **Evidence:** Ensemble/NWP best practices show that probabilistic forecasts improve decision guidance but the underlying deterministic skill is unchanged. Thus we dismiss this as a viable route to reduce error.

## 7. Lightning Data (Total Lightning / Lightning Assimilation)  
- **Novelty (Category A):** Lightning detection is an external data source (ground and satellite sensors) that most weather models do not use directly unless specifically assimilated. If the champion has not already used lightning strikes or flashes, this is new observational information. It can identify deep convection not seen by other sensors.  
- **Causality:** Lightning strongly indicates active convection and heavy rain. Assimilation studies show adding lightning observations (narrowed to convective regions) **improves** precipitation forecasts: e.g., assimilating total lightning increased 3–6 h precipitation POD by ~10%. This is a physically meaningful constraint on convective activity.  
- **2024 Coverage:** Many regions have ground lightning networks (e.g. EUCLID in Europe). Geostationary satellites now also measure lightning (e.g. GLM on GOES). Historical lightning data for 2024 are archived (though completeness varies).  
- **Spatial Representativeness:** Highly variable. Dense networks cover parts of Europe/Americas; Africa, Asia, and polar regions are sparse. Lightning doesn’t occur in stratiform or cold-season storms, so large areas/times see no data. The data are heavily biased toward strong convective cells.  
- **H1–H24 Usefulness:** Lightning is most useful for very short-range (nowcast) adjustment. Studies report clear improvement at 3–6 h, but effects drop off by 12+ h. By 24 h the gain is negligible (the cited work saw only ~1–2% POD gain at 24 h).  
- **Failure Modes:** Lightning alone does not predict storm movement or growth beyond a few hours. If no flashes occur, it offers no information. Assimilating lightning in a locked model requires sophisticated physics (nudging humidity) – not allowed here. Also, lightning can produce false-positives (especially in single networks).  
- **Expected Upside:** Could significantly reduce short-range precipitation misses (as shown, +10% detection at 3 h). However, it would mostly only help convective rain cases. Overall ODD nMAE (27.7%) might drop slightly in the 0–6 h window, but beyond that little difference.  
- **Feasibility:** Very low. Lightning assimilation requires modifying the model (imposing convection where flashes occur). Under locked rules, we cannot incorporate this data. It cannot be simply appended as a static feature without retraining or re-solving NWP physics.  
- **Evidence:** A CNR study found that “the assimilation of lightning data… shows an important improvement of the precipitation forecast” for 3–6 h (e.g. POD increase by 10% at 3h). Thus lightning is an attractive scientific candidate, but in a locked benchmark environment it is not actionable. 

**Summary:** The only truly new external information (AROME-Arctic, cloud optical, cloud motion, satellite data, lightning) all face serious obstacles under a locked benchmark. Satellite-derived cloud optical and motion cues are scientifically valuable (as cited) but mostly short-range and day-only, and cannot be used without reworking the model. AROME-Arctic offers new physics for high latitudes, but limited spatially. Lightning clearly boosts short-term convective forecasts, yet integration is infeasible without re-training. Fusion of satellite+NWP data (as in FuXi) is the most promising *in principle*, but is also an assimilation solution that cannot be plugged into the existing pipeline. Sequence forecasting or probabilistic outputs introduce no new data and cannot reduce error under fixed model constraints. In an adversarial light, none of these candidates appears capable of *plausibly breaking* the current error floor without violating the locked-benchmark rules or reintegrating data from scratch. The likely practical conclusion is that *no independent information source remains that can be easily added under the current constraints*; any recommendation requiring locked-model training changes must be rejected. 

**Sources:** Authoritative descriptions of AROME-Arctic and satellite observing techniques; research on cloud optical retrievals and motion vectors as forecast predictors; recent ML assimilation success (FuXi Weather); and studies on lightning-data assimilation improving short-range precipitation forecasts.
